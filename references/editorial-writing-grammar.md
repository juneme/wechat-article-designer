# Editorial Writing Grammar for WeChat

Use this reference for every new article and any substantial rewrite. It governs how the article thinks and moves before visual styling begins. These are composable editorial decisions, not a fixed article formula.

## Define the editorial promise

Before drafting, write one private sentence for each:

- **Reader situation**: the concrete moment, task, concern, or decision that brings the reader here;
- **Central friction**: the specific contradiction, cost, misconception, or unanswered question;
- **Editorial judgment**: what the article can responsibly establish from the available evidence;
- **Reader gain**: what the reader will understand, distinguish, decide, or do after reading;
- **Evidence boundary**: what remains assumed, incomplete, disputed, or outside scope.

The title, opening, section order, examples, and closing must serve this promise. Do not invent urgency, certainty, conflict, or benefit merely to create a stronger hook.

## Write the title

- Name the literal subject. Prefer a concrete tension plus a defensible judgment, method, or gain.
- Replace broad slogans and mood-only phrases with the decision, change, mechanism, or question the article actually addresses.
- Keep only one main promise. Put scope, audience, or conditions in a short deck when they cannot fit naturally.
- Do not use numbers unless the body contains the corresponding count, sequence, comparison, or evidence.
- Test the full title at mobile width; visual line breaks must not change its meaning.

## Open through recognition

A useful editorial opening often moves through three cognitive beats:

1. a familiar, observable situation;
2. the friction or consequence hidden inside it;
3. the article's central judgment and scope.

These beats may occupy two or three short paragraphs and may be reordered when the genre requires it. A notice can lead with the decision; an evidence report can lead with the finding; a reflective essay can lead with a precise scene. The opening is successful when readers know what is happening, why it matters, and what the article will establish.

Avoid throat-clearing, workflow narration, generic era-setting, stacked rhetorical questions, and claims that outrun the supplied material.

## Give every paragraph one cognitive action

Each paragraph should primarily do one job:

- state a judgment;
- explain a cause or mechanism;
- show an example, scene, or consequence;
- present evidence or attribution;
- qualify a claim or mark a boundary;
- connect the preceding idea to the next decision.

Split a paragraph when it changes jobs. Merge adjacent fragments when they repeat the same job without adding evidence or movement. A short paragraph is not automatically more readable; paragraph length should follow the idea.

Use transitions to express real logic such as cause, contrast, condition, sequence, or return. Do not decorate every paragraph with a label, quote, card, or summary sentence.

## Make headings perform work

- An H2 names the task or question of a chapter, not an abstract mood.
- An H3 exists only for a real concept, step, case, or subproblem inside that chapter.
- Parallel sections use parallel grammatical structure when their reader jobs are comparable.
- Do not create a heading for one orphan paragraph or use hierarchy merely to obtain a visual treatment.
- A visible index or chapter number must map to the actual section sequence.

Readers should be able to scan the headings alone and recover the article's reasoning path.

## Use lists and comparisons honestly

- Use an unordered list only for genuinely parallel items.
- Use an ordered list only when order, dependency, priority, or chronology matters.
- Give repeated records the same information schema; otherwise write separate prose or sections.
- In comparisons, name both the changed action and the resulting difference. Avoid contrasting vague labels with unlike evidence.
- Pair important numbers with units, timeframe, source, and interpretation where available.
- Never manufacture a matrix, score, stage, or before/after claim to justify a visual component.

## Control emphasis through editorial weight

Mark only the sentences that change interpretation, establish evidence, state a decision, or define an action. Those moments may receive stronger type or a semantic component later. Supporting explanation should remain open prose.

As a working ceiling, avoid more than about two strong emphasis moments in one mobile viewport and do not place strong components back to back without a quieter bridge. This is a density check, not a component quota.

## Close by resolving the opening

The closing should return to the opening friction and complete the reader's task. It may:

- state the final distinction or decision;
- compress the argument into a short parallel sequence;
- define the next concrete action and its condition;
- leave a bounded question that the evidence genuinely cannot settle.

Do not append a generic call to action, motivational slogan, or repeated summary. The closing should feel inevitable from the article, quieter than the opener unless action is the article's primary purpose.

## Editorial integrity

- Separate observation, inference, recommendation, and speculation.
- Attribute evidence and preserve supplied qualifiers.
- State assumptions, missing evidence, and applicability limits where they affect the conclusion.
- Preserve names, dates, figures, institutional terms, and quotations exactly unless the user authorizes revision.
- Do not use visual certainty to strengthen uncertain prose.

## Draft review

Before styling, read the text without components and confirm:

1. The title makes one defensible promise.
2. The opening establishes situation, friction, judgment, and scope without filler.
3. Each paragraph advances one main cognitive action.
4. Headings alone reveal the reasoning path.
5. Lists, numbering, and comparisons correspond to real relationships in the source.
6. The strongest sentences are few enough to form a clear emphasis hierarchy.
7. Evidence limits remain visible where they change interpretation.
8. The closing resolves the initial friction without a generic CTA.

If the unstyled article does not pass, revise the writing before composing its Visual DNA or HTML.
